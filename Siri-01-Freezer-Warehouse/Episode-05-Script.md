# Episode 5: Day 5
**Series:** Zombie Outbreak: 30 Days Trapped in a Freezer Warehouse

> Nombor dan masa ditulis dalam perkataan supaya suara AI membacanya dengan betul.

---

[SCENE: Top of the metal mezzanine staircase at night, Cal in his bulky dark freezer suit holding DEE (58, short grey hair, long coat) limp in his arms, MARCO (19) in the doorway gripping an ice scraper like a weapon]

Last time, Dee collapsed at the top of the stairs, and Marco saw the scratch on her arm. The one I've known about since Day Three.

Ten oh four P.M. Day five. Dee is burning hot in my arms, and Marco is holding an ice scraper. He's holding it the way you hold something you're about to use.

"How long, Cal?"

"Two days," I say. It's the truth. It just doesn't help.

[SCENE: The mezzanine office lit by a single desk lamp, DEE lying on a camp cot, MARCO standing back near the door with the ice scraper, Cal kneeling beside the cot reading a digital thermometer]

We get her onto the camp cot in the office. The first aid kit has a digital thermometer. It beeps.

Forty point one degrees.

Marco hasn't moved from the door. "She goes in the freezer. With the others. Now."

"She's not one of the others."

"Not yet."

He's right about the rule. A bite or a deep scratch, and you're dead in one to three hours. That's what we've seen. Ray took less than two.

And Dee is hot. Rule one: they hunt heat. If anything is still awake in this building, the warmest thing in it right now is the woman on this cot.

"Put the scraper down, Marco."

"You put her in the freezer, and I'll put it down."

[SCENE: Close-up of Cal's black digital wristwatch on his gloved wrist, the dim office behind it, his face reflected faintly in the watch glass as he counts]

Except I count everything. And the numbers don't work.

Dee was scratched on Day One. That's four days ago. Not three hours. Four days. Ninety-something hours, and she's still here, still breathing, still Dee.

"Marco," I say. "Look at the math. Something is keeping her alive."

He looks. I can see him doing it. The scraper drops a little. Not all the way.

[SCENE: Cal opening DEE's battered clipboard on the desk, pages of neat handwritten columns of dates, times and temperatures, the lamp lighting the paper]

Her clipboard fell on the stairs. I pick it up to put it somewhere, and that's when I see it.

It's not her fuel log. It's her own log. In her neat supervisor handwriting.

Day one, freezer, two hours. Day two, freezer, three hours. Day three, freezer, two and a half. Every night, while Marco and I were asleep, she was walking into the deep freeze and sitting with the frozen ones.

Day four. Nothing. Day five. Nothing.

[SCENE: Flashback-style image of DEE in her long coat sitting alone on an upturned crate inside the frost-covered deep freeze, breath steaming, frozen turned figures standing motionless in the background shadows]

The last two days, she was with us. Up on the plan, down at the chiller, running the plant room. Too busy to go and sit in the cold.

And yesterday, for the first time, she said she was too hot.

The cold slows them. Below minus ten, they stop. Dee figured out it slows whatever is inside her too. She just stopped doing it when we needed her.

[SCENE: Cal and MARCO hauling armfuls of frozen food boxes out of the freezer door, frost swirling around them, the dim shapes of frozen turned standing deep inside the freezer behind them]

"We cool her down," I say. "Fast."

Marco looks at me for a long time. Then he puts the scraper on the desk and follows me down.

We go into the freezer together. Walt is still standing where I left him three days ago. Lena too. We don't look at them. We grab frozen peas, frozen chicken, anything flat. Twenty boxes. Then twenty more.

[SCENE: DEE on her cot surrounded on all sides by a low wall of stacked frozen food boxes, frost forming on the cardboard, a desk fan blowing across her, Cal checking the thermometer]

We build a wall of frozen boxes around her cot. Peas under her arms, chicken at her neck. The desk fan blows the cold air across her.

It looks stupid. It looks like a fort a kid would make.

Eleven fifteen P.M. Thirty-nine point eight.

Midnight. Thirty-nine point two.

Two A.M. Thirty-eight point five.

[SCENE: Close-up of DEE's forearm resting on a frosted box, the dark scratch with thin grey frost-like lines around it, the lines visibly shorter and fainter than before, Cal's gloved finger pointing at the edge]

I hold the lamp over her arm. The grey lines around the scratch. Yesterday they reached her wrist.

Now they stop a finger's width short of it.

"Marco. Come here."

He comes. He looks. He doesn't say anything, but he sits down on the floor next to the cot, and he stays there for the rest of the night.

[SCENE: Early morning grey light in the office, DEE awake and propped up on her cot inside the frozen box wall, looking tired and guilty, Cal sitting on a crate facing her, MARCO by the window]

Day six. Six forty A.M. Dee opens her eyes and the first thing she says is, "You found the clipboard."

"You should've told us."

"And then what?" she says. "Marco puts me in the freezer. You let him, because you don't make those calls. You take the job, you don't take the lead. I've worked with men like you for twenty years, Cal."

That one lands. Because it's true. Danny used to say the same thing, nicer.

[SCENE: MARCO standing at the whiteboard writing a new rule in big marker letters, DEE watching from her cot, Cal leaning against the desk with his arms crossed]

Marco gets up and goes to the whiteboard. Under the old rules, he writes a new one.

Rule seven. Dee stays cold.

Then, under it, smaller. Somebody watches her. Always.

"That's not for me," he says to her. "That's for you. So if something changes, it's me who sees it. Not you alone in a freezer."

Dee looks at him for a long time. Then she nods.

[SCENE: DEE (58, short grey hair, long coat) sitting up on her cot wrapped in a blanket inside the wall of frosted boxes, holding a steaming cup in both hands, talking quietly, Cal and MARCO listening on crates]

Later, she tells us how she found out.

Day One, when the crowd came through the dock, she didn't run upstairs. She ran into the deep freeze and pulled the door shut, because it was the closest heavy door. She sat in there for two hours with the scratch on her arm burning like a hot wire.

And then it stopped burning.

"I thought I was dying," she says. "Then I realised I just wasn't getting worse."

She didn't know why. She only knew that every time she came out into the warm, the burning came back. So she kept going back in.

"Twenty years I've been telling drivers the cold keeps things fresh," she says. "I never thought it'd be me."

[SCENE: Cal inside the deep freeze grabbing frozen boxes, looking up at a wall thermometer that reads minus nineteen instead of minus twenty-two, frost on his hood, frozen turned figures standing still in the background]

Day six, two P.M. It's my turn for the box run.

I time it. Door open, grab, door shut. Forty seconds if I'm fast. Today I'm slow, and the thermometer by the door catches my eye.

Minus nineteen.

It's supposed to be minus twenty-two. Every time we open that door, warm air rolls in, and the compressors have to work harder to pull it back down. Harder means more diesel.

I look at Walt. He hasn't moved. At minus nineteen, he won't. But I know what minus ten looks like now. I watched twenty-six of them stop pushing at minus ten. I don't want to find out what minus nine looks like in here.

From now on, I write on the door: one trip, every four hours, no more.

[SCENE: The generator control panel on the office wall, a fuel gauge needle low in the orange zone, a second gauge showing chiller temperature at minus fifteen, Cal tapping the glass with a worried face]

Keeping her cold is the easy part. Keeping everything cold is the problem.

The chiller is still at minus fifteen, holding twenty-six of them paused. The freezer is holding Walt, Lena and four more. The boxes around Dee thaw in four hours, so every four hours, someone walks into the freezer for more.

And every bit of that cold comes from one diesel engine. Fuel gauge says just under two days.

[SCENE: Cal sitting at the dusty radio base station in the corner of the office at night, headphones on, slowly turning a large frequency dial, the orange display of his own handheld radio glowing beside it]

Day six, nine P.M. Dee is asleep, Marco is on watch, and I do something I should have done on Day One.

I scan the radio base station. Every channel. One by one.

Danny's rules. Keep moving. Stay dry. Count everything. If counting is the only thing I'm good at, I can count frequencies.

Static. Static. A tone. Static.

Then a voice.

[SCENE: Close-up of the radio base station's glowing display and speaker grille, faint waveform light flickering, Cal leaning in with one hand pressed to his headphones]

A woman. Calm. A little tired.

"This is Juno. If you can hear this, you're not alone. They follow heat. Stay somewhere cold. Stay quiet. I'll be back on this channel every night at nine. This is Juno."

Then a click. And it starts again. Same words. Same pause before her name. A recording on a loop.

I listen to it eleven times. I write down every word.

[SCENE: MARCO and Cal crowded around the base station, MARCO grinning wide for the first time, Cal holding the handset hesitantly, DEE awake on her cot in the background watching them]

Marco hears it and his whole face changes. "There's other people," he says. "There's other people in Ridgeport." He's thinking about the east side. About the river.

I press the handset. "Juno, this is Cal. Do you copy?"

Nothing. Just the loop.

"Careful," Dee says from the cot. "Anyone can make a recording. Don't tell it where we are."

Marco turns on her. "Why would anyone record a message telling people to stay safe, just to hurt them?"

"Because people come," Dee says. "People always come where there's help. And where there are people, there's food, and water, and fuel."

Nobody says anything after that. We all look at the little orange light on the base station, blinking. Somewhere out there, a woman left a message for strangers. Either she wants to save us, or she wants to find us. From here, those sound exactly the same.

I don't sleep much that night. I lie on the floor and listen to the loop until I know every breath in it.

[SCENE: The office at night on Day seven, Cal alone at the radio base station with a cold cup of coffee and the wristwatch showing nine, MARCO asleep on the floor, DEE asleep inside her frozen box wall]

Day seven. Nine P.M. exactly. I'm already sitting there.

The loop stops in the middle of a word. A long hiss. Then the same voice. But not recorded this time. Breathing between the words.

"Whoever said Cal last night. I heard you. Are you still there?"

I don't answer right away. I count to five first. Then I press the button.

"I'm here."

[SCENE: Cal gripping the radio handset with both hands, his face lit orange by the display, a tense expression, the dark warehouse visible through the office window behind him]

She asks how many of us. I say three. She asks where. I say a cold place off the highway, and nothing else. She doesn't push.

Then she asks something nobody should know to ask.

"Has anyone been scratched?"

I look at Dee, asleep in her wall of frozen boxes. I think about lying. Marco would lie. Dee would tell me to lie.

"One of us," I say. "Four days ago."

[SCENE: Close-up of the radio speaker grille in darkness, a single red signal light on the base station, the silhouette of Cal's hand frozen above the dial]

The silence goes on so long I think I've lost her.

Then she says, very quietly, "Four days. Then she's been staying cold."

I never said she.

Before I can ask, the lights in the office dip. Brown. Dim. The hum of the generator outside coughs once, catches, coughs again.

[SCENE: The office lights flickering to a dim brown, DEE waking up alarmed on her cot, MARCO jumping to his feet, Cal still holding the radio handset as the generator panel lights blink red]

Marco is on his feet. Dee is awake. On the panel, a red light I've never seen before is blinking.

And through the static, Juno's voice comes back, suddenly urgent.

"Cal. Listen to me. Whatever you do, don't let the power die."

*End of Episode 5.*
