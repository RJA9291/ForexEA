# Episode 4: Day 4
**Series:** Zombie Outbreak: 30 Days Trapped in a Freezer Warehouse

> Nombor dan masa ditulis dalam perkataan supaya suara AI membacanya dengan betul.

---

[SCENE: Wide view from the mezzanine office window at dawn, dozens of turned silhouettes shuffling out of the open loading bay toward a roaring generator shed outside, black exhaust smoke rising into a grey sky]

Last time, the power died and the backup generator kicked in. It kept the freezer cold, and it started calling every one of the turned for a kilometre around.

Six twenty A.M. Day three. Through the office window, I watch them walk out of Bay Four toward the noise. Out on the highway, more shapes are turning off the road. Coming here.

[SCENE: Inside the mezzanine office, DEE pale in her office chair pointing at a hand-drawn floor plan on the whiteboard, MARCO standing beside her, Cal in his bulky freezer suit studying the plan, red armband on his sleeve]

"We can't turn it off," Dee says. "No generator, no compressors. No compressors, the freezer warms up, and Walt and Lena wake up."

"So we make it quieter," I say.

Marco laughs, but nobody else does. Dee taps the plan. The generator shed sits against the north wall, right under the roof edge. Two big louvred doors face the highway. That's where the roar comes out.

"Cover the louvres," she says. "From the roof."

[SCENE: A storage cage in the ambient warehouse, stacks of thick silver insulated pallet covers folded on shelves, Cal and Marco pulling them down by flashlight]

In the ambient warehouse there's a cage full of insulated pallet covers. Thick silver blankets, made to keep frozen loads cold on the back of an open truck.

Made to stop heat. Good at stopping sound too, Dee says. She would know. She's ordered about a thousand of them.

We take eight, and a box of ratchet straps. Each cover weighs maybe six kilos. By the time we've carried them up the ladder, my arms are shaking inside the suit.

[SCENE: Cal pushing open a roof hatch at the top of a metal ladder, cold grey morning light and wind pouring in, the flat warehouse roof stretching out behind him]

The roof hatch opens onto wind and grey sky.

For a second, I just look. I can see the whole highway from up here. Cars stopped at angles, doors open. No lights in Ridgeport at all. Not one.

Danny used to say the first thing you do in a bad place is get high and look. Find the exits. Find the water. Count the people. He said it about camping. I don't think he ever pictured this.

Exits: the highway, and nothing else for nine kilometres. Water: whatever's in the vending machines. People: none that are still people.

Then I look down.

[SCENE: View straight down from the roof edge, the fenced generator shed below with black smoke pouring from its exhaust, around thirty turned pressed against the chain link fence, all facing the shed]

Thirty of them, maybe more, pressed against the fence around the generator shed. Shoulder to shoulder, faces to the noise, like people at a concert.

The fence is holding. For now. It's leaning.

I lie flat on the roof so my head isn't the warmest thing they can see.

[SCENE: Cal lying flat at the roof edge lowering a silver insulated pallet cover on a strap over the louvred doors of the generator shed, Marco behind him holding the strap, the crowd below not looking up]

We lower the first cover on two straps. It slides down over the top louvre and hangs there like a curtain.

The roar drops. Not much. A little.

Second cover. Third. Marco hooks the straps to an old satellite dish mount and ratchets them tight. Every click of the ratchet sounds like a gunshot to me. Down below, nobody looks up. They only care about the big noise.

Six covers. The roar turns into a rumble. A deep, heavy rumble, like a truck idling two streets away.

[SCENE: Close-up of a generator control panel on the mezzanine office wall, a temperature needle climbing into an orange zone, Dee watching it with a radio in her hand]

Then Dee comes on the radio.

"Cal. Engine temperature's climbing. You've choked its air."

Ninety degrees. Ninety-five. At a hundred and five, she says, it shuts itself down to protect itself. And then everything warms.

So we can't wrap it completely. We have to let it breathe.

[SCENE: Cal on the roof pulling one silver cover partly back to leave a gap at the bottom of the louvre, a thin stream of exhaust heat shimmering out, Marco counting on his fingers]

We pull the bottom of two covers back, a hand's width. Then two hands. Dee reads us the needle. Ninety-eight. Ninety-six. It holds at ninety-four.

Loud enough to keep the generator alive. Quiet enough that the highway can't hear it.

That's the deal now. Not silence. Just less.

[SCENE: View down from the roof, the crowd at the fence slowly losing interest, some turned drifting away toward the highway, others turning back toward the open loading bay]

Down at the fence, they stop pressing. Some drift back toward the highway. A few stand still.

And a lot of them turn around and walk back into Bay Four. Back to the dock, where the truck engines are still warm.

That's problem two. Bay Four doesn't close. It's been broken since before any of this started. And as long as it's open, the warehouse is their warehouse.

[SCENE: The mezzanine office, Cal pointing at the chiller on the whiteboard floor plan, DEE looking up from her chair with a tired half smile, MARCO leaning in]

"The chiller," I say. "We put them in the chiller and we make it cold."

Dee thinks about it for a long time. Then she nods. "The chiller compressors were built for blast chilling. From the plant room, I can push it down to minus fifteen. Forty minutes, maybe."

"And it burns more diesel," Marco says.

"It burns more diesel," Dee agrees. "Everything does."

[SCENE: A small portable electric fan heater sitting alone at the far end of the green-lit chiller aisle, an orange extension cord trailing back across the wet floor toward the door]

We need bait. Something warm. Rule one: they hunt heat first.

In the drivers' lounge there's a little fan heater. We run it on a long orange extension cord, through the chiller, to the very back wall. Marco can switch the circuit on from the office panel.

Somebody has to carry it down there, through the dock, past them.

Marco says he'll do it. I tell him no.

[SCENE: Cal in his bulky dark freezer suit walking slowly through the crowded loading dock carrying the heater, turned figures standing close on both sides, none of them turning toward him, his breath held]

Here's what I noticed on the roof. They looked at the generator. They looked at the warm trucks. They never looked at me, and I was right above them.

The suit doesn't just keep the cold out. It keeps me in.

So I walk through the dock with the heater in my arms. Hood up, face down, breathing through my nose. They're close enough to touch. One of them stands so near I can see the frost of my own suit reflected in his eyes.

He doesn't turn. I'm just a cold shape walking past.

Forty metres of dock. I count my steps so I don't count them. Fifty-two steps. On step thirty, a woman in a hi-vis vest steps right into my path, and I have to stop and wait, nose to shoulder, while she decides where to go. It takes nine seconds. It feels like nine minutes.

She drifts left. I keep walking.

[SCENE: Cal crouched behind a dairy rack inside the chiller holding his radio, the heater glowing orange at the far end of the aisle, turned figures pouring in through the sliding door toward it]

I set the heater down at the back wall and hide behind the last rack.

"Now, Marco."

The fan clicks on. Warm air. A small, orange glow.

It takes eleven seconds. Then they start coming in through the sliding door. Three. Ten. Twenty. They walk past my rack without a glance, all of them going to the warm thing at the end, like moths.

[SCENE: Cal slipping out through the narrow side door of the chiller into the corridor, Dee at the far end of the chiller in her coat pressing a large red button that slides the heavy chiller door shut]

I count twenty-six. Then I slip out the side door, the one I used yesterday, and pull it shut.

On the radio: "Door closing."

Dee is at the sliding door controls. I asked her to stay in the office. She didn't. The heavy door rolls shut on its track with twenty-six of them inside.

"Plant room," she says, already walking. "Give me two minutes."

[SCENE: Close-up of a chiller temperature display on a wall panel, numbers dropping, the heavy sliding door behind it bowing slightly as something pushes from the other side]

Plus two. Zero. Minus three.

Then the door starts to move.

Rule five. They don't open doors, but they push. Twenty-six of them pushing at once. The steel bows in the middle. The track creaks.

Minus five. Minus six.

[SCENE: Cal and MARCO bracing a loaded pallet jack against the bowing chiller door, both in freezer suits, leaning their full weight into the handle, the door shuddering]

Marco runs down with a pallet jack loaded with frozen chicken. We jam it against the door and lean on it with everything we have.

The door shoves back. A hand's width. Then less.

Minus eight. Minus nine.

The pushing slows. The way Walt slowed. Then it stops.

Minus ten. Silence on the other side of the door.

[SCENE: Inside the loading dock, MARCO on a quiet electric forklift sliding a pallet stacked with frozen boxes against the bottom of the broken half-open Bay Four roller door, Cal guiding him with hand signals]

The dock is almost empty now. Four of them left, standing by the trucks.

Marco climbs on a forklift. Electric, almost silent. Before he starts, he reaches under the dash and pulls a wire. The reversing beeper.

"I'm not Ray," he says.

He sets three pallets of frozen goods against the bottom of Bay Four, like bricks. The door can't come down, but now nothing can come in under it either. The four left inside, we lead into the freezer, one at a time, with the heater. Slowly. Carefully.

By noon, the warehouse is ours.

[SCENE: The mezzanine office at night, rows of water bottles counted on the desk, the whiteboard updated with a fuel tally, Marco asleep, Cal writing numbers, Dee watching from her chair]

Day four is quiet. We count everything.

Fifty-one bottles of water. Generator fuel down faster than Dee said, because of the chiller. Maybe two and a half days left.

That night, Marco and I sit by the window and split a frozen pizza we warmed on the generator exhaust pipe through the roof hatch. It's the best thing I've ever eaten.

"My mom lives in Ridgeport," he says, out of nowhere. "East side. Near the river."

I look at the city. No lights on the east side. No lights anywhere.

"She's tough," he says. "She's tougher than me."

"Then she's fine," I say. I don't know if it's true. He doesn't ask me if it's true. That's how we both know.

Day five, Dee won't put on her freezer suit. She says she's too hot. She's never too hot. She sleeps a lot. When she's awake, she works like nothing is wrong.

[SCENE: The metal staircase from the mezzanine, DEE collapsing at the top step with her clipboard falling from her hand, Cal lunging up the stairs toward her]

Day five, ten P.M. Dee stands up to check the fuel gauge.

She gets three steps. Then her legs just stop.

I catch her at the top of the stairs. She's burning up. I can feel the heat of her through two layers of suit.

[SCENE: Close-up of DEE's forearm with her sleeve fallen back, a dark scratch clearly visible with grey frost-like discoloration spreading from it, MARCO standing frozen in the doorway behind, staring at it]

Her sleeve has fallen back. All the way back.

The scratch is there. Clear this time. Dark. And the skin around it has gone grey, spreading out in thin lines, like frost on a window.

Behind me, I hear Marco stop breathing.

He's in the doorway. He's seen it. He's looking at her arm, then at me.

"Cal," he says, very quietly. "How long have you known?"

*End of Episode 4.*
