It's one thirty-five in the morning. I'm standing inside a freezer at minus twenty-two degrees, and I'm wearing a canvas work jacket.

The door behind me doesn't lock from the inside. On the other side of it are the people I clocked in with three and a half hours ago.

They stopped banging about ten minutes ago. I don't know if that's good news.

Here's what I do know. Out there, they kill you in seconds. In here, the cold kills you in about an hour.

My name is Cal Mercer. I'm a temp. This was supposed to be my third night shift at Harlan Cold Logistics.

Let me tell you how I ended up in here.

Ten oh two P.M. Three and a half hours earlier.

I clock in. The building is huge, the size of three football fields. It sits on an industrial road nine kilometres outside Ridgeport, right next to the highway.

There are three of us on nights. Dee Okafor is the supervisor. She's fifty-eight and has worked here for twenty years. She knows the building better than the people who built it.

Marco is nineteen. He drives the forklift like he's in a racing game, and he's talked nonstop since I met him.

The work is simple. Trucks come in, frozen food goes out. I move pallets from the dock into the deep freeze. That's minus twenty-two. Dee makes me wear a freezer suit every time I go in.

"Five minutes in there without a suit," she told me on my first night, "and your fingers stop listening to you."

I'm about to find out she was being generous.

Eleven twenty P.M. The last truck is forty minutes late. It backs into Bay Four, the broken one, the door that never fully closes. The reversing alarm beeps the whole way in.

The driver climbs down. His name is Ray. His right hand is wrapped in a gas station napkin that has gone dark.

"Some guy at the Exit Nine pumps," he says. "Just grabbed me. Bit me. Like an animal."

He laughs as he says it. He looks grey.

Dee walks him up to the break room on the mezzanine and makes him sit. Marco turns on the radio in the truck cab to kill the silence.

It isn't music. It's a voice reading the same message on a loop. Residents of Ridgeport are advised to stay indoors. Do not approach individuals who appear ill.

Marco turns it off. Nobody says anything.

Eleven forty-eight P.M. Dee calls nine-one-one. It rings twice, and then a recording says all lines are busy.

She tries again. Same recording.

She tries Ray's company. No answer.

I check my phone. One bar, then none. The highway is usually loud all night. Right now it's quieter than I've ever heard it.

Ray is shivering now, a heavy shake from his shoulders down. Dee wraps a moving blanket around him. He keeps saying he's cold, he's so cold.

I remember that later. He kept saying he was cold.

Twelve forty-one A.M. Ray stops shaking.

Dee checks his pulse. She checks it again, longer this time. Then she pulls the blanket up over his face, slowly, like you do.

Marco is crying, quietly, with his back to us.

Dee goes into supervisor mode. "Nobody touches him. We wait for the ambulance. We keep working." She says it like the ambulance is coming.

So we keep working, because none of us knows what else to do.

Twenty-four minutes later, I'm in the dock area, and I hear Marco scream.

One oh five A.M. Ray is standing at the top of the mezzanine stairs.

He's not looking at anything. His head turns, slowly, side to side. It looks like he's smelling the air.

Then he goes for Dee.

He doesn't run, exactly. He falls toward her, fast and heavy. Dee gets an arm up. I hear fabric tear.

I grab the first thing I can reach, a pallet jack, and I shove it between them. Steel handle, two hundred kilos. Ray goes backwards down three steps.

He gets up like he didn't feel it.

Dee is holding her forearm. She's pressing her sleeve down hard.

"I'm fine," she says. "I'm fine. Go."

One oh nine A.M. That's when I hear it. The truck's reversing alarm is still going. Beep. Beep. Beep. It's been going for almost two hours, because nobody turned off the engine.

Out on the highway, people are walking toward that sound. Twenty, maybe thirty of them.

They're walking toward Bay Four, the door that never fully closes.

"Marco!" Dee yells. "Office! Now!"

Marco grabs Dee and pulls her up the side stairs to the mezzanine office. It has a steel door. They make it.

I don't. Ray is between me and the stairs.

The first ones are ducking under the Bay Four door. They move toward us, toward Marco's forklift with its warm engine, toward me.

I run the only way that's open: deeper into the building.

The chiller first, plus two degrees. I go through the plastic strip curtain. Behind me they slow down, just a little, like they're walking through water.

I notice that. Then I stop thinking, because the freezer door is in front of me.

I haul it open. Minus twenty-two hits me like a wall. I'm inside. I pull the door. It's a heavy door with a slow hydraulic closer, and it closes on its own time.

Something gets through the gap before it seals.

It's a woman in a gas station uniform. The name tag says Lena. Exit Nine pumps. Maybe the one who bit Ray. Maybe not.

She comes at me. I back up between the pallets. My heel hits a box and I go down hard on the frozen floor.

She's three metres away, then two. And then she slows down.

Her arms are still reaching, but they're slow, like a video buffering. Her fingers curl, then don't uncurl. Each step takes longer than the last.

Within a minute she's standing perfectly still, frost forming on her eyelashes.

The cold stopped her.

I lie there breathing. The pounding on the door starts up from outside, a lot of hands. I wait for the door to give.

It doesn't. Over ten minutes, the pounding slows down, and then it stops.

So I do what I always do. I count.

Fourteen aisles. Forty pallets per aisle. Frozen peas, frozen fries, frozen chicken. Enough food to feed a small town for a year, and none of it can keep me warm.

The freezer suits are in the locker room, on the other side of the building. The emergency release button is next to the door, which only matters if I want to open it.

I find a stack of flattened cardboard boxes and wrap them around my legs with pallet tape. Cardboard is a terrible blanket. It's still better than canvas.

I tuck my hands into my armpits. I keep my back against a pallet, as far from Lena as the aisle allows. And I keep my eyes on her.

She doesn't move. She doesn't blink. Ice keeps growing on her collar, thin and white, like feathers.

Okay, I tell myself. Okay. She's frozen. She's done.

That's my mistake. I believed that for about twenty minutes.

One forty A.M. I can't feel my toes. My hands shake so badly it takes me three tries to press the button on my radio.

"Dee. Marco. Anyone."

Static. Then Marco, whispering.

"Cal? Cal, you're alive. Listen. They're all at the freezer door. All of them. They're just standing there. Waiting."

"For what?"

"I don't know, man. It's like they can feel you in there."

They hunt heat. Ray was cold, so he got up looking for warm. They walked past the chiller. They walked to the freezer door, the only place in this building where something warm is still breathing.

Me.

I check my watch. I've been in here for twenty-six minutes. By Dee's rule, my fingers should have stopped listening to me twenty minutes ago.

I figure I have maybe forty minutes before the cold finishes what they started.

Then I hear a sound inside the freezer. It's small, a crackle, like ice breaking on a pond.

I look up.

Lena is still standing three metres from me, frozen. But the frost on the side of her face, the side facing me, is melting.

And she just turned her head toward my breath.
